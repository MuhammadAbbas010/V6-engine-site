01 — Everything at once

Break it.

Every part in front of you is a settlement between four things that cannot all be satisfied: heat, mass, sealing and time. Make the piston lighter and it cracks. Make the valve bigger and the chamber loses its shape. Make the block stiffer and it becomes too heavy to hang off the front of a car.

What follows is the same engine, laid out in the order you would actually take it apart.

02 — The exploded view

The whole of it.

Read it from the middle outwards. The crankshaft lies along the spine. Rods reach up out of it into pistons; pistons run in liners inside the block; the heads cap the liners; the camshafts sit on top of the heads; the intake fills the valley between the two banks and the exhaust leaves from the outside of each.

Nine subsystems, colour-held together by nothing but bolts and oil pressure.

03 — Block & crankcase

The argument that holds.

The block does one job: keep every other part in the same place relative to every other part, while the whole assembly is being shaken thousands of times a second and heated unevenly from the inside.

The 60° angle is not styling. It is the angle at which a three-throw crank fires a six cylinder engine at even 120° intervals, and it is narrow enough to fit across an engine bay sideways. The bores are iron liners because aluminium is far too soft to be a running surface, and the four bulkheads down the centre hang the crankshaft in a line straight enough to measure in microns.

04 — Rotating assembly

Three throws, six cylinders.

This is the part that makes a V6 a V6. Three crank pins, spaced 120° apart, each carrying two connecting rods side by side — one from the left bank, one from the right. Six cylinders, three throws, one forging.

The counterweights are not there to add momentum. They exist to cancel the force of the pistons and rods trying to tear themselves out of the engine, which at 6000 rpm is on the order of two tonnes per piston, reversing direction 200 times a second.

At the back, the flywheel; at the front, the pulley and its rubber damper. Between them the crank twists and untwists like a torsion bar every time a cylinder fires.

05 — Pistons & rods

Straight line into a circle.

The piston travels 86 mm and stops. Twice per revolution. The rod, 150 mm between centres, is what converts that into rotation — and because it swings as it goes, the piston does not move as a clean sine wave. It spends longer near the bottom of the bore than the top, which is why combustion pressure has time to do useful work.

Three rings do the sealing: two compression rings above, holding roughly 60 bar of burning gas out of the crankcase, and one oil control ring below, scraping the bore clean on the way down so the oil never reaches the fire.

06 — Cylinder heads

Where power is decided.

Displacement tells you how much air the engine can theoretically swallow. The head decides how much it actually gets. Four valves per cylinder in a pentroof chamber, spark plug in the middle so the flame front reaches every bore wall at the same moment.

Between the head and the block sits a shim of layered steel less than four millimetres thick that has to seal combustion pressure, coolant and oil across the same joint, through every heat cycle for the life of the engine. When people say an engine "blew a head gasket," this is the part that gave up.

07 — Valvetrain

Twenty-four valves, on time.

Four camshafts, each turning at exactly half crankshaft speed, because a four-stroke cycle takes two revolutions to complete. Every lobe is a small essay: when the valve starts to open, how far it lifts, how long it stays there, and how gently it is put back down.

Nothing pulls a valve shut. A spring does it, and the spring is in a race with the cam. Wind the engine past what the spring can follow and the valve floats — it stops obeying the lobe, power disappears, and in a bad case the piston finds it.

Watch the intake and exhaust valves in this model: they open and close in the real firing order, 1-4-2-5-3-6.

08 — Intake

Air, delivered in turn.

One throttle plate meters the air for all six cylinders. Behind it, the plenum sits in the V as a shared reservoir — six cylinders gulp one after another, and without that volume each gulp would rob its neighbours.

The runners are tuned, not merely routed. When an intake valve slams shut, a pressure wave runs back up its runner, reflects off the plenum, and returns. Get the length right and it arrives just before that valve closes on the next cycle, cramming in extra air at no cost. Long runners tune for low rpm, short ones for high.

09 — Exhaust

Getting out of the way.

An exhaust system is not a drain. Each primary pipe is sized and matched in length so that the departing slug of gas leaves a low pressure wave behind it, which arrives back at the valve during overlap and physically pulls the next intake charge in. Get it wrong and one cylinder blows its exhaust down another one's pipe.

Where the three primaries of each bank merge, an oxygen sensor reads what actually burned, and the ECU trims the injectors accordingly. It is the feedback loop that makes modern fuelling work.

10 — Fuel & ignition

Milliseconds and degrees.

The fuel rail holds petrol at a regulated pressure so that identical injector opening times give identical fuel to all six cylinders. The injector opens for perhaps two to twelve milliseconds per cycle. That number — the pulse width — is the entire fuel system, and it is what the fuel control at the bottom of this page is scaling.

Ignition happens a few degrees before top dead centre, because flame takes time to spread. Fire exactly at the top and peak pressure arrives too late to push on anything.

Run the engine and watch the flash inside each cylinder: that is the power stroke, firing in order, and it dims as the tank runs down.

11 — Drive & accessories

What the engine owes.

An engine never gets to keep all of its own output. Off the front of the crank, a belt takes power to the water pump, the alternator and the air conditioning compressor. Off the back, the flywheel hands what is left to the clutch.

Between them, the timing cover hides the chain that keeps four camshafts in step with the crankshaft. If that chain jumps a single tooth, valve timing shifts, and this engine stops being an engine.

12 — Put it back

Now watch it work.

Assembled and running. The pistons, rods, camshafts and all twenty-four valves are solved from real geometry, not looped animation — change the speed and every part still agrees with every other part.

How this model moves

Nothing here is keyframed. One number drives the whole engine — crank angle, from 0° to 720°, one complete four-stroke cycle.

Piston position comes out of the slider-crank relation: the distance along the bank axis is s = d + √(L² − r² − dx² + d²), where d is the projection of the crank pin onto the bank axis, r is the 43 mm crank radius and L the 150 mm rod. Because it is solved rather than animated, the rod length stays constant to within a millionth of a millimetre at every angle, and the measured stroke is exactly 86.0 mm.

Each connecting rod is aimed by pointing it from its gudgeon pin at its crank pin, so it can never separate from either. Camshafts turn at half crank speed. Valve lift is a cosine lobe 240° wide in crank degrees, centred on that cylinder's intake or exhaust event, offset by its place in the 1-4-2-5-3-6 firing order — which is also what sequences the combustion flashes.

The fuel model is deliberately simple and deliberately visible: burn rate scales with rpm, and below 18% in the tank the mixture goes lean, so rpm sags and oscillates until it stalls.
